Legal English for Lawyers: The Vocabulary a Law Degree Rarely Teaches

Knowing the law and being fluent in the register lawyers use to practice it are two different skills.

By the Lexfall team · Updated September 2026

A law degree, including a master's in law (LL.M.), teaches you the law. It rarely teaches you the language lawyers use to practice it. You can know exactly what a force majeure clause is supposed to do and still hesitate over "shall" versus "will" in a contract, or freeze for a second while translating a judge's question into the answer you actually mean.

Where it actually bites

Contract drafting. Legal English has its own vocabulary of precision: "notwithstanding," "subject to," "without prejudice," "time is of the essence," "indemnify and hold harmless." Each phrase carries a specific legal effect, and the wrong one changes what the clause does. There is also a real shift toward plain-English drafting in many jurisdictions, so you need both registers and a sense of when each is expected.

Client correspondence. The tone that reassures without overpromising is its own register: "we would advise caution here," "our recommendation, subject to further review, is..." Get this wrong and you sound evasive, or you sound like you're promising an outcome no lawyer should promise.

Negotiation. Softening language does real work: "we would be prepared to consider...", "that's not something we could agree to as drafted, but...". It's a different skill from knowing strategy. It's knowing how to say the strategy without sounding too soft or too aggressive.

Oral advocacy. Courts have their own formal register. If English isn't your first language, the gap between reading legal English and producing it live, under pressure, is real. It needs deliberate, repeated exposure, not one read-through of a case.

Ten phrases worth having ready

Contract precision

  • "Notwithstanding the foregoing..."
  • "Time is of the essence."
  • "Subject to the terms set out herein..."
  • "Indemnify and hold harmless against..."

Client correspondence

  • "We would advise caution before proceeding."
  • "Our recommendation, subject to further review, is..."

Negotiation

  • "We would be prepared to consider..."
  • "That's not something we could agree to as drafted, but..."
  • "Let's come back to that point once we've addressed..."

Formal register

  • "Without prejudice to our position that..."

From phrases to vocabulary

Phrases carry the register. Vocabulary carries the precision, and much of it comes from exposure rather than coursework.
