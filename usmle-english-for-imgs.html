USMLE English Communication for IMGs: The Vocabulary Nobody Tests You On

The exams test what you know. Nobody tests how you say it.

If you're an international medical graduate, you've probably already beaten the exam that was supposed to be the hard part. Step 1, Step 2, maybe Step 3, hundreds of hours of question banks and review books, all in a language that isn't the one you grew up thinking in. Most people underestimate how much harder medicine is in a second language, then act surprised when the real gap shows up somewhere the exams never tested: the interview room, the ward, five minutes with a patient who is scared and doesn't want a lecture.

Where the real gap shows up

Residency interviews. You can explain a differential perfectly and still sound stiff answering "tell me about a time you disagreed with an attending." That's a register problem, not a medicine problem. Nobody teaches this vocabulary explicitly: "what draws me to this specialty is...", "I take initiative in...". You either absorbed it or you didn't.

Clinical communication. Talking to an attending isn't the same English as talking to a patient, and both differ from what you write in the chart. "The patient reports intermittent epigastric pain" is chart language. "Can you tell me where it hurts, and when it started?" is patient language. Hedging language, "I'd like to rule out," "it's possible that," "we should keep an eye on," is one of the most underrated skills in clinical English. Get it wrong and you sound alarmingly certain or alarmingly vague.

The wards. Handoffs, curbside consults, the fast, idiomatic English between colleagues. Nobody writes a textbook chapter on this register. It's absorbed, usually over years, unless you build it on purpose.

Ten phrases worth practicing this week

Hedging and reassurance

  • "I'd like to rule out a few things before we worry."
  • "It's possible that this is related to..., but let's confirm first."
  • "We'll keep an eye on this and reassess in a few days."

Patient-facing

  • "Can you walk me through when this started?"
  • "I want to make sure I understand what's bothering you most."

Interview and self-description

  • "What draws me to this specialty is..."
  • "I take initiative in situations where..."
  • "A time I advocated for a patient was..."

Handoff and colleague-facing

  • "Nothing acute overnight, stable, plan is to..."
  • "Curbside question: do you think this needs imaging before we discharge?"

What actually helps

Not more general vocabulary. The specific, field-shaped vocabulary of medicine in English, at the register you'll actually use it in: patient-facing, attending-facing, interview-facing.
